1: Preventive Driving Techniques

Preventive driving allows you to acquire information from your surroundings and react rapidly to any unpredictable incident. Based on three core principles.

👁
Vision

Look further ahead as speed increases. Check mirrors regularly. Look sideways frequently. Control dead angles by turning your head.

🧠
Anticipation

Never take other road users for granted. Predict the worst. Adapt speed to circumstances. Make yourself visible and signal clearly.

📐
Space

Manage the space in front, behind, to the sides, and when stopping. Minimum 2-second front gap; 2–3 m when stopped.

1.1 Vision techniques

  • See at distance — as your speed increases you must look further ahead; you need more time and distance to react.
  • Look sideways frequently — to react to events at the edge of the road.
  • Check rear-view mirrors frequently — even when not manoeuvring:
    • Urban areas and main roads with intense traffic: every 5–10 seconds
    • Low-traffic main roads and dual carriageways: every 10–15 seconds
  • Turn your head briefly to look through the windows — to control the mirror's dead angle.
Key principle

You should not only look at the vehicle ahead — gather all the remaining information to process it and react accordingly.

1.2 Anticipation techniques

  • Never take other road users for granted — predict the worst that can happen and prepare for it. This reduces your effective reaction time.
  • Adapt speed to the circumstances — allows you to anticipate hazardous situations and stop safely.
  • Make yourself seen and clear — signal all manoeuvres clearly, use the lighting system, and avoid other drivers' dead angles.
The preventive driver

A preventive driver is aware that mistakes happen to all road users — and anticipates the risk rather than assuming others will always act correctly.

1.3 Space management

Front space
Keep at least a 2-second gap from the vehicle ahead in urban areas and on highways. Increase by at least 1 second in bad weather, fatigue, or when being tailgated.
Rear space
If the vehicle behind is too close: increase front gap (1 second), signal earlier, and brake progressively and in time.
Side space — Uncertainty zone
Avoid invading the space occupied by other road users — they may make sudden, unpredictable moves into it.
Stopping space
Keep 2 or 3 metres from the vehicle ahead when stopped — to avoid impact if they reverse, to overtake if needed, and to avoid rear-ending them.
If you are last in a queue
  • Increase the gap ahead to 5 or 6 metres
  • Keep your foot on the brake
  • Watch the rear-view mirror — do not trust anyone within reach
  • If a vehicle approaches fast from behind: engage 1st gear and prepare to move forward
  • Maintain these distances until you have 2 or 3 cars behind you

Benefits of efficient driving

🛡
Increased safety

Efficient driving requires applying preventive driving techniques — making you a safer driver overall.

💰
Reduced costs

Lower fuel consumption and reduced wear extend the vehicle's useful life and lower repair and maintenance costs.

😌
Greater comfort

Avoiding bursts of acceleration and sudden braking keeps a steady speed and reduces passenger discomfort.

🔇
Noise reduction

Smoother driving produces less engine and tyre noise — improving quality of life in cities.

2.1 Starting the vehicle and moving off

General rule
Ignite the engine without accelerating — modern vehicles do not require revving to start.
Petrol engine
Move off immediately after the engine is ignited.
Diesel engine
Wait a few seconds after starting before moving off — to allow proper lubrication.

2.5 Other recommendations

  • Avoid driving in urban centres and intense traffic where maintaining a cruising speed is difficult.
  • Switch off the engine when stationary for more than 60 seconds to avoid idle fuel consumption.
  • Set air conditioning to 23°C — the best balance of comfort and efficiency. Remember: A/C increases fuel consumption.
  • Use the vehicle's own cooling devices — lowered windows increase aerodynamic resistance and fuel consumption.
  • Do not use petrol with an inferior octane rating to that recommended by the manufacturer.
  • Avoid over-loading — arrange load correctly. The roof rack increases consumption even when empty.
  • Have the vehicle regularly checked (tyres, air filter, etc.) to avoid unnecessary breakdowns and excess emissions.
  • Do not change the exterior of the vehicle as its aerodynamic resistance may be affected.
2.2–2.4: Gears & Driving Speed

Correct gear selection is the single most impactful efficient driving skill — tested heavily in the theory exam.

Moving up through gears

  • Use 1st gear as little as possible — change to 2nd after just 2 seconds or 6 metres.
  • Change gears up as soon as you can — accelerate smoothly and progressively.
  • You do not need to use gears in exact sequence — skipping gears is efficient and correct.
  • Use the highest gear possible (4th or 5th) even in urban centres — keep the engine running at low revolutions.

2.3 Changing down through gears

Zero fuel at deceleration

When driving in gear at more than 20 km/h and you stop accelerating, the fuel consumption is zero. This is the most efficient way to decelerate.

  • Apply the brakes smoothly and progressively.
  • When changing down, the later the better — no need to change gears sequentially going down either.

2.4 Driving speed

Both fuel consumption and contaminating emissions increase with speed because higher speed increases rolling resistance and especially air resistance. Drive at a moderate, steady speed.

How to maintain a cruising speed

Avoid unnecessary bursts of acceleration and sudden braking. Keep an average cruising speed most of the time. This is achieved through the vision, anticipation, and space techniques of preventive driving — increasing your gap allows you to maintain flow without braking cycles.

2.6: Measures to Reduce Contamination

Motor vehicles produce air pollution (harmful to health) and noise pollution (may cause nerve disorders). This section covers the DPF and legal prohibitions.

Active Particulate Filter (FAP / DPF)

Fitted in the exhaust pipe of diesel engines. Retains solid particles from combustion. When full, particles are burned off in a process called regeneration. Ideal regeneration temperature: 600°C.

DPF recommendations
  • Avoid short trips — filter never reaches 600°C and saturates early.
  • Do not switch off the engine during regeneration (warning light will show).
  • Use Low SAPS oil — specifically for diesel engines with a particle filter.
  • Follow all manufacturer maintenance instructions.
  • At least every 1,500 km: drive for half an hour at over 2,500 rpm.
Warning

Interrupting the regeneration cycle can solidify partially burned particles, blocking the DPF permanently and requiring expensive replacement.

Prohibited vehicle operation

Circulation of motor vehicles and mopeds is strictly forbidden when they:

  • Lack a suitable complete silencer, or use roaring cutout pipes
  • Are fitted with straight exhaust systems without proper silencing devices
  • Lack the device that prevents spraying of non-burned fuel
  • Expel harmful fumes that hinder visibility or cause danger
  • Produce unnecessary noise (horn, loud music, etc.) or electromagnetic disturbances
  • Produce noises, fumes, or gases above the legal limits
Legal consequence

Police authorities not only sanction offenders — they may also seize the vehicle.

3: UVAR & Environmental Labels

Urban Vehicle Access Regulations and the DGT environmental classification system — a major exam topic.

3. Urban Vehicle Access Regulations (UVAR)

UVAR definition
"A measure regulating vehicle access to the road infrastructure of an urban area."

Municipalities with more than 50,000 inhabitants, island territories with more than 20,000, and any municipality exceeding pollution limits must adopt sustainable urban mobility plans by 2023. These introduce low-emission zones (ZBE), cycling promotion, public transport improvements, and EV charging infrastructure.

3.1 Low Emission Zones (ZBE / LEZ)

Delimited areas where access, circulation, and parking restrictions apply to improve air quality and reduce greenhouse gas emissions.

Sign R-120

Entry prohibited to vehicles based on their environmental badge or other environmental criteria. Conditions are specified on supplementary panels or signs.

Badge placement
  • Placement is voluntary, but cities with mobility ordinances may restrict vehicles without one
  • Must be placed on the lower right corner of the front windshield (or clearly visible location if no windshield)
  • Can be purchased at: Post Offices, CETRAA workshop network, authorised administrative managers, or the IDEAU-TO — presenting the vehicle registration certificate

3.2 Environmental classification — DGT labels

The National Air Quality and Atmospheric Protection Plan (Plan Aire) classifies vehicles by their polluting potential. The DGT badge contains: EURO emission level (4, 5, or 6), licence plate number, fuel type, and a QR code.

0
Zero Emissions Label

Battery electric (BEV), extended-range electric (REEV), plug-in hybrid (PHEV) with ≥40 km electric range, fuel cell vehicles. Most favourable

E
ECO Label

PHEV with <40 km range, non-plug-in hybrids (HEV), CNG, LNG, LPG vehicles — provided they also meet Label C criteria.

C
Label C

Petrol cars/vans from January 2006; diesel from September 2015. Petrol and diesel buses/trucks from 2014.

B
Label B

Petrol cars/vans from January 2001; diesel from 2006. Buses/trucks from 2006.

No label

Vehicles without any label are the most polluting (older/higher emission) and are excluded from ZBE zones.

Label order (most → least environmentally favourable): Zero → ECO → C → B → no label
